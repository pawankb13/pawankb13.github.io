# Mental Accounting and Pricing

Behavioural Economics for Managers · Module 4

Why a rupee is not always a rupee, and how prices and menus use that

Note:


**The idea** "A rupee is not always a rupee." In standard economics every rupee is the same as every other rupee. In real life, people treat a rupee differently depending on where it came from, what it is meant for, and how it is paid.

**What the module covers.** Two parts. The first half is about how people keep score with money: mental accounts, the value of a deal, reference prices and split prices. The second half is about how firms design around this: when and how customers pay, decoy options, middle options and the number of options on a menu.

**Why "pricing".** Every concept in this module ends in a pricing or menu decision a manager has to make. The theory matters because it changes revenue, renewals and trust.

## What this module is about
### Money, prices and menus as people actually see them

> Standard economics treats every rupee as the same, and judges a price only by what the product is worth. People do not.

They put money in mental accounts. They judge a price against what they expected to pay. They feel the pain of paying more with cash than with a card. They choose differently when the menu changes, even if their options do not.

Note:
**Standard economics makes two assumptions.**

- Money is fungible. A rupee from salary, a bonus or a gift can be used for anything, so its source should not matter.
- A price is judged only against the value of the product. If a product is worth ₹1,000 to me and costs ₹800, I gain ₹200, and nothing else matters.

**What people actually do.** The slide lists four patterns, and each one becomes a concept later.

- **They put money in mental accounts.** Money for rent, money for fun and money for petrol are kept apart in the mind. This is mental accounting (Concept 1).
- **They judge a price against what they expected to pay.** The same price feels like a bargain in one place and a rip-off in another. This is transaction utility and reference prices (Concepts 2 and 3).
- **They feel the pain of paying more with cash than with a card.** How and when we pay changes how much we spend and use. This is payment decoupling (Concept 5).
- **They choose differently when the menu changes.** Adding or removing an option can change which option people pick, even if no one wanted the added option. This is the decoy effect, the compromise effect and assortment design (Concepts 6 to 8).

None of this means people are foolish. These are shortcuts that usually work. But firms can design prices around them, for good or for bad.

## Before we start: two evenings at the theatre
### Would you still buy a ticket?

|||

### Evening 1

You plan to see a play. The ticket costs ₹500. At the door you find you have **lost a ₹500 note**. Do you still buy a ticket?

|||

### Evening 2

You bought a ₹500 ticket earlier. At the door you find you have **lost the ticket**. Do you buy another one?

Note:
**About this slide.** This is the opening problem of the module. It comes from Kahneman and Tversky (1984), who used a $10 theatre ticket. Here it is set at ₹500. Students should answer before seeing the results on the next slide.

**The key point to notice.** In both evenings the loss is exactly ₹500. Nothing about the play, the price or the person has changed. Only the form of the loss is different: cash in one case, a ticket in the other.

**Answer.** In standard economics the two evenings are the same. In both, you are ₹500 poorer and you face the same choice: pay ₹500 to see the play, or go home. So the share of people who buy should be the same.

**What people actually do.** In Kahneman and Tversky (1984), 88% said they would still buy a ticket after losing the cash, but only 46% would buy again after losing the ticket.

**Why.** People keep a mental "theatre" account. The lost note is charged to general cash, so the play still costs ₹500. The lost ticket is charged to the theatre account, so a second ticket makes the evening feel like it costs ₹1,000, which is too much for one play.

**The lesson.** The label on a loss, not its size, changes the decision. This is mental accounting, the first concept of the module.

## The link to Module 3
### The same value function, applied to each account

> Mental accounting decides which account a gain or a loss goes into. The value function from Module 3 then decides how it feels.

2. **The value function decides how it feels.** It is judged as a gain or loss from a reference point, with losses weighing about twice as much as gains (loss aversion, λ about 2.25).

- **Reference points.** Here they become reference prices.
- **Loss aversion.** Paying feels like a loss, so the pain of paying matters.
- **The four rules of segregating and integrating.** Thaler's hedonic editing rules: segregate gains, integrate losses, integrate a small loss with a larger gain, and segregate a small gain from a larger loss. These explain why firms bundle costs and split out bonuses.

## Concept 1: Mental accounting
### Definition

> Mental accounting is the set of mental operations people use to organise, evaluate and keep track of money. Richard Thaler (1999).

Money is fungible: any rupee can replace any other. In our heads it is not. We label money by where it came from and what it is for.

Note:
**The definition** of mental accounting, from Richard Thaler (1999): the set of mental operations people use to organise, evaluate and keep track of money.

**Fungibility.** In economics, money is fungible. Any rupee can replace any other. A rupee won in a lottery buys the same rice as a rupee earned at work.

**What people actually do.** In our heads, money is not fungible. We label it in two ways.

- **By source.** Salary feels like "hard-earned money". A bonus, a gift or a win feels like "free money" and is spent more easily.
- **By use.** We keep budgets for rent, food, fun and savings, and we do not like to move money between them.

**Everyday examples.**

- People keep savings in a fixed deposit at 7% while carrying a credit card balance at 36%. In pure money terms they should pay off the card.
- People spend a tax refund on a holiday but would not take the same sum from their savings for that holiday.

**Who is Thaler.** Richard Thaler of the University of Chicago developed mental accounting from 1980 onwards and won the Nobel Prize in Economics in 2017.

## Mental accounting: the theory
### Three parts, Thaler (1999)

| Part | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Coding outcomes | Each gain or loss is judged with the value function | A ₹500 loss feels worse than a ₹500 gain feels good |
| Assigning to accounts | Money is put into labelled budgets | Rent, food, fun, savings |
| Balancing accounts | Accounts are closed daily, weekly or per trip | "I have spent my fun money for the month" |

Note:
**What this slide shows.** The three parts of mental accounting, from Thaler (1999).

**1. Coding outcomes.** Each gain or loss is judged with the value function from Module 3. A loss of ₹500 hurts more than a gain of ₹500 pleases. This step also includes whether outcomes are combined or kept apart, which follows the hedonic editing rules.

**2. Assigning to accounts.** Money and spending are put into labelled budgets, such as rent, food, fun and savings. Each account has its own limit. When the fun account is empty, people stop spending on fun even if they have money elsewhere.

**3. Balancing accounts.** Accounts are opened and closed over a period: a day, a week, a month or a trip. Examples:

- A taxi driver with a daily income target stops early on a busy day, because the daily account is full.
- A tourist who has "already spent the holiday budget" refuses a cheap but good meal on the last day.

**Why this matters.** The period and the labels change decisions. A firm that knows which account a purchase falls in, and when that account is closed, can predict how customers will respond.

## Mental accounting: the theory
### Standard economics against mental accounting

|||

### Standard economics

Money is **fungible**. A rupee from salary, a bonus or a gift has the same value and the same use.

|||

### Mental accounting

Money is **labelled**. The source and the account change how it is spent, even when the total is the same.

Note:
**What this slide shows.** A direct comparison between standard economics and mental accounting.

**Standard economics.** Money is fungible. A rupee from salary, a bonus or a gift has the same value and the same use. If income rises by ₹10,000, spending should rise by the same amount whatever the source.

**Mental accounting.** Money is labelled. The source and the account change how it is spent, even when the total is the same.

**A simple illustration.** Two people each get an extra ₹10,000 this month.

- One gets it as a salary increase. Most of it goes to bills and savings.
- The other gets it as a festival bonus. More of it goes to clothes, gifts or a trip.

Their total wealth is the same, but their spending is not.

**The point for managers.** Labels are cheap to change. A firm can change how customers spend just by changing what a payment is called. The next slide shows this in a controlled experiment.

## Mental accounting: an experiment
### Epley, Mak and Idson (2006). Students got $25 in cash, then could buy items in the lab.

|||

# $2.43

Spent when the $25 was called a "rebate"

|||

# $11.16

Spent when the same $25 was called a "bonus"

|||

# 4.6×

Same money, different label

Note:
**What this slide shows.** A lab experiment by Epley, Mak and Idson (2006), published in the Journal of Behavioral Decision Making.

**The set-up.** Students were given $25 in cash. The only difference between groups was the word used to describe the money.

- One group was told the $25 was a "rebate", money being returned to them.
- The other group was told the same $25 was a "bonus", extra money.

They could then buy snacks and office items in the lab.

**The result.**

- Rebate group: spent on average **$2.43**.
- Bonus group: spent on average **$11.16**.
- The bonus group spent about **4.6 times** as much.

**Why this is strong evidence.** The money was real, the amount was the same and people were assigned at random. Only the label changed. So the label caused the difference.

**Context.** The study was motivated by the 2001 US tax rebate. The government wanted people to spend the money to support the economy. The research suggests that calling it a "bonus" would have raised spending.

## What the experiment shows
### The label decides the account

|||

### "Rebate"

Feels like your own money coming back. It goes into the savings account, so most of it is kept.

|||

### "Bonus"

Feels like extra money. It goes into the spending account, so more of it is spent.

Note:
**"Rebate".** A rebate feels like your own money coming back to you. It returns you to where you were before. So it is placed in the savings or "current wealth" account, and most of it is kept.

**"Bonus".** A bonus feels like extra money on top of what you had. It is a gain. So it is placed in the spending or "windfall" account, and more of it is spent.

**The link to Module 3.** A rebate is coded as a return to the reference point. A bonus is coded as a gain above the reference point. The reference point decides the account, and the account decides the spending.

**Business uses.**

- Cashback credited as "bonus" wallet money tends to be spent quickly on the same platform.
- Employers who call a payment a "performance bonus" rather than a "salary arrear" change how it is used by staff.
- Governments that want people to spend, for example during a slowdown, should call a transfer a bonus, not a refund.

## Business case: cheaper petrol, more premium petrol
### Hastings and Shapiro (2013), *Quarterly Journal of Economics*

**The setting:** Purchases by 61,494 US households. Petrol comes in regular, midgrade and premium grades.

**The puzzle:** In autumn 2008, during a deep recession, petrol prices fell. Households cut spending almost everywhere, yet their share of midgrade and premium petrol rose by almost 4 percentage points.

**The finding:** When petrol prices rise, households switch to regular petrol far more than income effects can explain. They treat "petrol money" as its own account.

Note:
**What this slide shows.** A field study by Hastings and Shapiro (2013), published in the Quarterly Journal of Economics, one of the top journals in economics.

**The data.** Petrol purchases by 61,494 US households from a large retailer's loyalty card records. In the US, petrol comes in three grades: regular, midgrade and premium. Premium costs more and, for most cars, gives no real benefit.

**The puzzle.** In autumn 2008, the US was in a deep recession. Petrol prices fell sharply. Households cut spending almost everywhere. Yet their share of midgrade and premium petrol rose by almost 4 percentage points.

**What standard economics predicts.** Cheaper petrol makes households a little richer. That income effect should cause only a very small switch to premium petrol, much smaller than what was seen.

**The finding.** The switch was far larger than any income effect. The same happened in reverse when prices rose: households moved to regular petrol much more than income could explain. They treat "petrol money" as its own account. When petrol is cheap, the petrol account has money to spare, and it is spent on better petrol, not moved to food.

**Why this case matters.** It shows mental accounting in real purchases with real money, at large scale, not only in lab studies.

## Why would a household in a recession buy more premium petrol when petrol becomes cheaper?

Where else is money labelled this way in a business?

Note:
**About this slide.** This question applies mental accounting to the Hastings and Shapiro petrol case. The puzzle is that households in a recession, cutting spending almost everywhere, bought more expensive petrol when petrol became cheaper. The follow-up question moves from households to firms, to show that labelled budgets are common in business too.

**Answer.** The household keeps a "petrol account" with a monthly budget. When the price of petrol falls, the same budget buys the usual litres and leaves money over inside that account. The leftover money is not moved to food or rent. It stays labelled "petrol", so it is spent on a better grade of petrol.

**Why this is not rational.** Money saved on petrol is fungible. In a recession, a rational household would spend the saving where it matters most, such as food or debt. Hastings and Shapiro (2013) show that the switch to premium grades was far larger than any income effect could explain.

**Other places money is labelled this way.**

- **Department budgets.** A team that underspends its travel budget often spends the rest before year end, rather than return it, because the money is labelled "travel".
- **Gift cards and wallet credits.** Money in an app wallet is spent more freely than cash, and often on items the buyer would not have chosen.
- **Bonuses and refunds.** A tax refund or a festival bonus is spent on treats, while salary is kept for bills.
- **Fuel and meal cards from employers.** Employees treat them as separate money, even though the employer could pay the same amount as salary.

## Concept 2: Acquisition and transaction utility
### Definition, Thaler (1985)

> **Acquisition utility** is the value of the good minus the price paid. **Transaction utility** is the pleasure or pain of the deal itself: the reference price minus the price paid.

We buy the product, and we also "buy" the feeling of a good deal or a bad deal.

Note:
The definitions of acquisition utility and transaction utility, from Thaler (1985).

**Acquisition utility.** The value of the good to you minus the price you pay. This is what standard economics calls consumer surplus. If a jacket is worth ₹3,000 to you and costs ₹2,000, acquisition utility is ₹1,000.

**Transaction utility.** The pleasure or pain of the deal itself. It is the reference price minus the price paid. If you expected the jacket to cost ₹2,500 and it costs ₹2,000, transaction utility is ₹500. You feel you got a good deal.

**The key line.** "We buy the product, and we also buy the feeling of a good deal or a bad deal."

**Why this matters.**

- People sometimes buy things they do not need, because the deal is too good to miss. Transaction utility is high even when acquisition utility is low.
- People sometimes refuse things they value, because the price feels unfair. Transaction utility is negative and outweighs acquisition utility.

Sales, coupons and "was" prices work mainly through transaction utility.

## Acquisition and transaction utility: the theory
### Total value of a purchase has two parts

$$\text{Acquisition utility} = V - p$$

$$\text{Transaction utility} = p^{*} - p$$

$V$ is the value of the good to you. $p$ is the price paid. $p^{*}$ is the reference price: what you think it should cost here.

Note:
**What this slide shows.** The two parts of the value of a purchase, as simple formulas.

**Acquisition utility = V − p**

- V is the value of the good to you, in rupees.
- p is the price you pay.

**Transaction utility = p* − p**

- p* is the reference price: what you think the good should cost in this place.
- p is the price you pay.

**Total value of the purchase.** Thaler combines the two. The total is acquisition utility plus transaction utility. A purchase can be attractive because the product is valuable, because the deal is good, or both.

**A worked example.** A pair of shoes is worth ₹4,000 to you. The usual price is ₹3,500. Today it is on sale at ₹3,000.

- Acquisition utility = 4,000 − 3,000 = ₹1,000.
- Transaction utility = 3,500 − 3,000 = ₹500.

If the shop had simply charged ₹3,000 all year, the reference price would be ₹3,000 and transaction utility would be zero. Same shoes, same price, less pleasure. This is exactly what J.C. Penney found later in this concept.

**Where p* comes from.** The reference price depends on the seller, the setting, past prices and printed "was" prices. Concept 3 studies it in detail.

## The theory in an experiment: beer on the beach
### Thaler (1985). The most you would pay for the same cold beer.

|||

# $2.65

From a fancy resort hotel (median)

|||

# $1.50

From a small, run-down grocery store (median)

Note:
**What this slide shows.** Thaler's (1985) "beer on the beach" study, the classic test of transaction utility.

**The set-up.** Participants imagined lying on a hot beach. A friend offers to get them a cold bottle of their favourite beer. They are asked the most they would pay. Half were told the beer would come from a fancy resort hotel. The other half were told it would come from a small, run-down grocery store.

**The result.**

- From the resort hotel, the median answer was **$2.65**.
- From the grocery store, the median answer was **$1.50**.

**Why this is striking.** The beer is the same. It is drunk on the same beach. The buyer never enters the hotel or the shop, so there is no ambience to enjoy. Acquisition utility should be the same in both cases. Yet people would pay about 75% more if the seller was a resort.

**The point.** What changed was the reference price p*. People think a resort is allowed to charge more. A high price there feels fair. The same price at a grocery store feels like a rip-off.

**Note on the figures.** These are from the early 1980s, so the prices look low today. What matters is the gap between the two.

## What the beer shows
### Same beer, same beach, different reference price

> The beer gives the same acquisition utility in both cases. What changes is $p^{*}$, the price people think is fair for that seller.

A high price at a resort does not feel like a rip-off. The same price at a grocery store does.

Note:
**Same acquisition utility.** The beer, the beach and the thirst are the same. So V − p would be the same at any given price.

**Different reference price.** People hold different ideas of a fair price for each seller. For a resort, p* is high. For a grocery store, p* is low. At a price of $2.50:

- From the resort, p* − p is about zero or positive. The deal feels fair.
- From the grocery store, p* − p is negative. The deal feels like a rip-off.

So people refuse to pay $2.50 to the grocery store even though they would happily pay it to the resort.

**Business lessons.**

- **Context sets the fair price.** Airports, cinemas and five-star hotels can charge more for the same water bottle because buyers expect it.
- **A low-cost seller cannot easily charge high prices,** even for the same product, because buyers judge it against a low reference price.
- **Firms invest in setting,** such as store design and brand image, partly to raise the reference price.

Note:
**What this slide shows.** A replication of the beer study by Shah, Shafir and Mullainathan (2015), published in Psychological Science.

**Why a replication matters.** The original study is from the 1980s and used a small sample. Repeating it decades later checks that the effect is real.

**The result.**

- **Higher-income participants** showed the classic effect. They would pay more at the resort: on average $6.09, against $4.21 at the grocery store.
- **Lower-income participants** would pay about the same in both places.

**Why lower-income people behaved differently.** The authors argue that scarcity changes how people think about value. When money is tight, people judge a price against what else the money could buy: a meal, a bus fare, part of a bill. That trade-off is the same whatever the seller, so the seller does not matter.

**Two lessons.**

- The transaction utility effect replicates, at least for people who are not under financial pressure.
- It is not universal. People under scarcity may be closer to the standard economic model on this point.

**For managers.** Price framing tricks may work less well with price-sensitive customers who think hard about trade-offs.

## J.C. Penney's prices were lower. Why did customers leave?

And when does chasing deals hurt the customer?

Note:
**About this slide.** This question applies acquisition and transaction utility to the J.C. Penney case. Lower prices should have attracted customers, but sales fell by a quarter. The question asks students to explain this with the two parts of value. The follow-up turns the lens on customers: transaction utility is a real feeling, but it can also lead people astray.

**Answer.** J.C. Penney raised acquisition utility but removed transaction utility.

- **Acquisition utility** is value minus price. Lower everyday prices raised it.
- **Transaction utility** is the reference price minus the price paid. Coupons and "was" prices gave shoppers a high reference price, so each purchase felt like a win. With everyday "fair" prices, there was no reference price to beat, so there was no feeling of a deal.

Customers did not only buy shirts. They bought the pleasure of a bargain. Rivals such as Kohl's and Macy's still offered that pleasure, so shoppers moved there. Sales fell 24.8% in a year and the policy was reversed.

**When chasing deals hurts the customer.**

- When they buy things they do not need because the discount feels good.
- When the "was" price is inflated, so the deal is not real.
- When they spend time and fuel travelling to a sale that saves less than it costs.
- When they choose a worse product because it is on offer.

Transaction utility is a real feeling, but it can lead people to pay for the deal rather than the product.

## Concept 3: Reference prices
### Definition

> A reference price is the price a buyer uses to judge whether a price is high or low.

It can be **internal**, held in memory, or **external**, shown at the point of sale. It is the $p^{*}$ in transaction utility.

Note:
**Two kinds.**

- **Internal reference price.** Held in memory: the last price paid, the usual price, or what the buyer thinks is fair.
- **External reference price.** Shown at the point of sale: an MRP, a "was" price, a competitor's price or a price-match promise.

**The link to Concept 2.** The reference price is p* in transaction utility. So anything that raises the reference price makes the actual price feel like a better deal.

**The link to Module 3.** A reference price is a reference point for prices. Paying more than the reference price feels like a loss. Paying less feels like a gain. Because losses weigh more than gains, a price above the reference price is resisted strongly.

**Why it matters.** Buyers rarely know the true value of a product. The reference price fills the gap, and firms can shape it.

### Where buyers get their reference price

| Type | Source | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Internal | The last price paid | "Tea was ₹15 last week" |

| External | A printed or "was" price | MRP ₹2,999, now ₹1,499 |

**Internal: the last price paid.** "Tea was ₹15 last week." If it is ₹20 today, it feels expensive, even if ₹20 is normal elsewhere.

**Internal: the usual or fair price.** "A haircut should be ₹200." This comes from experience and from what others pay.

**External: a printed or "was" price.** "MRP ₹2,999, now ₹1,499." The printed number becomes the reference, so ₹1,499 feels like a ₹1,500 saving, whether or not anyone ever paid ₹2,999.

**External: a competitor's price.** The same phone on another site. Comparison sites and price-match promises make this reference very easy to find.

**For managers.** Internal references are hard to change in the short run. External references are easy to change, which is why firms use them so often. This is also where the risk of misleading customers is highest.

**In India.** The MRP is printed by law on packaged goods. It is meant to be a maximum price, but it also works as an external reference price. Many sellers offer "discounts" on MRPs that are set high.

## Reference prices: why cues work
### Anderson and Simester (2003), *Harvard Business Review*

### Buyers know few prices

**Cues fill the gap.** Since buyers cannot judge most prices directly, they look for signals. Common cues are:

- A price ending in 9, such as ₹499.
- A price-match promise.
- A "was" price next to the current price.

**The risk.** A cue can be used even when the price is not good. The buyer trusts the signal and does not check. This is where pricing cues can mislead.

## Reference prices: an experiment
### Anderson and Simester (2003), three field experiments on price endings

**The test:** Retailers changed price endings at random, so the same item was offered at a price ending in 9 or at a nearby price.

**The finding:** A price ending in 9 raised demand in all three experiments.

**The twist:** The effect was stronger for new items than for items sold in earlier years. Customers know less about new items, so they lean more on price cues.

Note:
**What this slide shows.** Three field experiments on price endings by Anderson and Simester (2003), published in Quantitative Marketing and Economics.

**The test.** A US mail-order catalogue retailer printed different versions of its catalogue and sent them to random groups of customers. The same item was shown at a price ending in 9, such as $39, or at a nearby price, such as $34 or $44.

**The finding.** A $9 ending raised demand in all three experiments. In some tests, items priced at $39 sold more than the same items at $34, even though $34 is cheaper.

**The twist.** The effect was stronger for new items than for items the retailer had sold in earlier years.

**Why the twist matters.** Customers know less about new items, so they have no internal reference price. They lean more on cues. For familiar items, customers already know roughly what the price should be, so the cue adds less.

**Why this is good evidence.** Customers were randomly assigned to catalogue versions, so the difference in sales was caused by the price ending, not by other factors.

**For managers.** Price cues matter most where customers are least informed: new products, rare purchases and complex products.

## Business case: the power of a "Sale" sign
### Anderson and Simester, catalogue and grocery field studies (2001)

Note:

## If a "Sale" sign alone raises demand, why not put one on every product?

## Concept 4: Partitioned pricing
### Definition

> Partitioned pricing splits one price into a base price and one or more mandatory charges, such as delivery, packaging or a platform fee.

When the extra charges appear only step by step, at checkout, it is called **drip pricing**.

Note:
**The definition of partitioned pricing**: splitting one price into a base price and one or more mandatory charges, such as delivery, packaging or a platform fee.

**Examples.**

- Food delivery: food price plus delivery fee plus platform fee plus packaging charge.
- Airline tickets: base fare plus taxes plus convenience fee plus seat selection.
- Hotel bookings: room rate plus a "resort fee".
- Online shopping: product price plus shipping.

**Drip pricing.** When the extra charges appear only step by step, at checkout, after the buyer has already decided, it is called drip pricing. This is the more harmful form, because the buyer sees the full price only at the end.

**Partitioned versus drip.** Partitioned pricing shows all the parts. Drip pricing hides some parts until late. The next slides show that both raise demand, and that drip pricing raises it most.

**The regulatory angle.** India's CCPA guidelines of 2023 list drip pricing as a dark pattern.

## Partitioned pricing: the theory
### Morwitz, Greenleaf and Johnson (1998)

|||

### What happens

Buyers anchor on the base price and do not add the surcharges fully. They recall a lower total cost, and demand rises.

|||

### A puzzle from Module 3

Thaler's Rule 2 says to combine losses. Partitioning works for another reason: **attention**. The first number seen becomes the anchor.

Note:
**What this slide shows.** The theory of partitioned pricing, from Morwitz, Greenleaf and Johnson (1998), the first study of the topic.

**What happens.** Buyers anchor on the base price, the first and biggest number they see. They do not add the surcharges fully. As a result:

- They recall a lower total cost than they actually paid.
- Demand is higher than with one all-in price of the same total.

**A puzzle from Module 3.** Thaler's hedonic editing rule 2 says people prefer losses to be combined. One payment of ₹1,180 should hurt less than ₹1,000 plus ₹180. So why does splitting the price help sellers?

**The answer: attention.** The hedonic editing rules describe how people feel when they see all the numbers clearly. Partitioned pricing works through attention and anchoring. The first number seen becomes the anchor. Later numbers get less attention and less weight. The buyer never fully adds them up.

**For managers.** Splitting a price can raise demand, but only because customers underestimate the total. If customers later notice, they may feel cheated. The next slide models this with a simple weight.

## Partitioned pricing: a simple model
### A $10.00 shelf price, with 7.375% sales tax added at the till

$$\text{Price paid} = 10.00 \times 1.07375 = 10.74$$

$$\text{Price felt} = 10.00 + w \times 0.74, \quad 0 < w < 1$$

If shoppers give the tax little weight $w$, they react as if the price were close to $10.00.

Note:
**What this slide shows.** A simple model of how people react to a price with a hidden tax, using the real setting of the Chetty study on the next slide.

**The setting.** In many US states, shelf prices do not include sales tax. The tax is added at the till. Here the shelf price is $10.00 and the sales tax is 7.375%.

**Price paid.** 10.00 × 1.07375 = $10.74. The tax is about $0.74.

**Price felt.** 10.00 + w × 0.74, where w is the weight shoppers give to the tax, between 0 and 1.

- If w = 1, shoppers fully notice the tax. They react to $10.74.
- If w = 0, shoppers ignore the tax. They react to $10.00.
- If w = 0.5, shoppers react to about $10.37.

**What the model predicts.** If w is small, shoppers react as if the price were close to $10.00. Showing the tax-inclusive price on the shelf would make w = 1 and demand would fall.

**Why this model is useful.** It turns "people do not notice" into a number that can be measured. Chetty, Looney and Kroft estimated w in a real store, as the next slide shows. Their estimate implies that shoppers gave the tax well under half the weight of the shelf price.

## Partitioned pricing: an experiment
### Chetty, Looney and Kroft (2009), *American Economic Review*

**The setting:** A US grocery store where shelf prices exclude the 7.375% sales tax. The tax is added only at the till.

**The experiment:** For three weeks in 2006, the store showed tags with the tax-inclusive price on 750 taxed products.

**The result:** Demand for those products fell by about 8%, compared with other products and nearby stores. Shoppers had been under-reacting to a cost they could not see.

Note:
**What this slide shows.** A field experiment by Chetty, Looney and Kroft (2009), published in the American Economic Review. Raj Chetty is one of the best-known economists working today.

**The setting.** A large US grocery store. Shelf prices exclude the 7.375% sales tax. The tax is added only at the till, so shoppers must work out the full price themselves.

**The experiment.** For three weeks in 2006, the store put new tags on 750 taxed products in some aisles, such as cosmetics and hair care. The tags showed the price with the tax included, next to the usual price.

**The result.** Demand for those products fell by about 8%, compared with the same products in nearby stores and with other products in the same store.

**What this means.** The tax was always charged. Nothing changed in the actual price. Only its visibility changed. Yet sales fell 8%. So before the change, shoppers had been under-reacting to a cost they could not see.

**Why it matters.** This is strong evidence that hidden or separate charges change behaviour. It supports the partitioned pricing theory with a real store and real money. It also matters for tax policy: taxes shown on the shelf affect demand more than taxes added at the till.

## StubHub earned more with fees shown late. Should it have kept the change?

And is a separate delivery fee always drip pricing?

Note:
**About this slide.** This question asks students to weigh a short-run revenue gain against long-run costs, using the StubHub experiment. The follow-up asks students to separate fair partitioned pricing from drip pricing, which is the line drawn by India's CCPA guidelines.

- It is shown clearly, early, next to the base price.
- It reflects a real cost or a real choice, such as express delivery.
- The buyer can see the total before deciding.

The same fee becomes drip pricing when it appears for the first time at the last step of checkout.

## Concept 5: Payment decoupling
### Definition

> Payment decoupling weakens the link between paying and consuming: by paying earlier, later, by card, or for a bundle.

The looser the link, the less the "pain of paying" is felt at the moment of use.

Note:
**The definition of payment decoupling**: weakening the link between paying and consuming.

**Four ways to decouple.**

- **Paying earlier.** Prepaid passes, annual memberships, advance tickets.
- **Paying later.** Credit cards, buy now pay later, monthly bills.
- **Paying by card or wallet** instead of cash.
- **Paying for a bundle.** One price for many items or many uses.

**The pain of paying.** Paying feels like a small loss. Each time we hand over money, we feel it. When payment and use are tightly linked, the pain is felt at the moment of use, and it reduces enjoyment and use.

**The effect of decoupling.** The looser the link, the less the pain of paying is felt when we consume. This has two effects.

- People spend more, because paying hurts less.
- People may use less of what they prepaid for, because the cost is no longer in mind.

**Where it is used.** All-inclusive holidays, buffets, subscriptions, airline lounges, Netflix and theme park passes are all designed so that consumption feels free at the moment of use.

## Payment decoupling: the theory
### Prelec and Loewenstein (1998): the pain of paying

| How you pay | Link to what you consume | Pain at the moment of use |
|---|---|---|
| Cash, item by item | Tight | High |
| Card, paid next month | Weaker | Lower |
| Prepaid wallet or pass | Paid before use | Low |
| Subscription or bundle | One price for many uses | Lowest |

Note:
**What this slide shows.** The pain of paying, from Prelec and Loewenstein (1998), "The Red and the Black", Marketing Science.

**The idea.** People keep a "double entry" mental account. Each use of a product brings pleasure, but thinking about its cost brings pain. How tightly the two are linked depends on how we pay.

**The table, row by row.**

- **Cash, item by item.** The link is tight. You hand over money as you consume. The pain at the moment of use is high. Example: paying for each ride at a fair.
- **Card, paid next month.** The link is weaker. You do not see money leave. The pain is lower. Example: shopping on a credit card.
- **Prepaid wallet or pass.** Payment happens before use. At the moment of use, the pain is low. Example: a metro card.
- **Subscription or bundle.** One price covers many uses. Each use feels free. The pain is lowest. Example: a streaming service or an all-you-can-eat buffet.

**What Prelec and Loewenstein found.** People prefer to prepay for things they will enjoy, such as holidays, so the cost does not spoil the experience. They dislike paying after use for things already consumed.

**For managers.** Moving customers down this table usually raises spending and satisfaction at the moment of use. But it can also lead customers to overspend.

## Payment decoupling: the theory
### Payment depreciation, Gourville and Soman (1998)

|||

### Right after paying

The cost is fresh. People use what they paid for, to "get their money's worth".

|||

### As time passes

The memory of the payment fades. The sunk cost matters less, and use falls.

Note:
**What this slide shows.** Payment depreciation, from Gourville and Soman (1998), Journal of Consumer Research.

**Right after paying.** The cost is fresh in mind. People use what they paid for so they "get their money's worth". This is the sunk cost effect. Economically, a sunk cost should not matter, but people feel it.

**As time passes.** The memory of the payment fades. The sunk cost matters less and less. Use falls.

**An example.** Someone pays ₹12,000 for a year of gym membership in January. In January and February they go often, because the payment feels recent. By June the payment feels distant, and they go rarely.

**Why it is called depreciation.** Like an asset that loses value over time, the mental weight of a payment declines over time.

**For managers.** If use drives renewal, a firm wants the payment to feel recent. More frequent billing, or reminders of value, keep use up. This idea is tested in the health club case later in this concept.

## Payment decoupling: an experiment
### Soman and Gourville (2001), a Shakespeare festival, *Journal of Marketing Research*

**The data:** Ticket and attendance records from a 1997 summer Shakespeare festival.

**The comparison:** Some theatregoers bought a ticket for a single play. Others paid once for a bundle of four plays.

**The result:** Single-play buyers had a no-show rate below 1%. Bundle buyers had a no-show rate of 21.5%. The bundle blurred what each play cost, so missing one hurt less.

Note:
**What this slide shows.** A field study by Soman and Gourville (2001), Journal of Marketing Research.

**The data.** Ticket and attendance records from a 1997 summer Shakespeare festival in the United States.

**The comparison.**

- Some theatregoers bought a ticket for a single play.
- Others paid once for a bundle of four plays.

**The result.**

- Single-play buyers had a no-show rate below 1%.
- Bundle buyers had a no-show rate of 21.5%.

**Why.** With a single ticket, the buyer knows exactly what that evening cost. Missing it means wasting a clear, known sum. With a bundle, the cost of each play is blurred. Missing one play does not feel like losing a definite amount. So the sunk cost pressure to attend is weaker.

**The authors called this transaction decoupling.** Bundling separates the price of each part from its use.

**Related evidence.** The same authors found a similar pattern with ski passes: buyers of a multi-day pass were more likely to skip a day than buyers of single-day tickets.

**For managers.** Bundling can raise sales, but it may lower use. If use matters for repeat purchase, word of mouth or spending on extras such as food and parking, low use is a cost to the firm.

## Business case: the health club bill
### Gourville and Soman (1998)

**The club:** A health club that billed members twice a year.

**The pattern:** Attendance jumped in the month after each bill arrived, then fell steadily until the next bill.

**The lesson for managers:** Members who stop using a service are less likely to renew. Gourville advised managers to encourage use in every period, because use drives renewal.

Note:
**What this slide shows.** The health club study by Gourville and Soman (1998), on payment depreciation.

**The club.** A health club that billed its members twice a year.

**The pattern.** Attendance jumped in the month after each bill arrived. Then it fell steadily month by month until the next bill. Then it jumped again.

**The explanation.** Right after the bill, the payment is fresh in mind. Members go to the gym to get their money's worth. As months pass, the payment fades from memory, and so does the urge to attend.

**The lesson for managers.** Members who stop using a service are less likely to renew. A member who has not been to the gym for three months sees the renewal bill as money for nothing. So the club's real problem is not attendance as such. It is renewals.

**Gourville's advice.** Encourage use in every period, because use drives renewal. Ways to do this include more frequent billing, regular reminders of what members get, and usage-based rewards.

**Where this applies.** Gyms, clubs, subscription apps, software licences and online courses all face the same pattern.

The question is how the billing schedule changes use and renewal. The follow-up asks students to think from the customer's side.

**Answer. The monthly bill gets more renewals.**

- **Payment depreciation.** Right after paying, the cost feels fresh and members go to the gym to "get their money's worth". As time passes, the memory of the payment fades and use falls (Gourville and Soman 1998).
- **With one annual bill,** members go often in the first month or two, then stop. By renewal time many have not been for months, so the membership feels useless and they do not renew.
- **With a monthly bill,** each payment is a fresh reminder. Use stays steadier through the year. Members who use the gym renew.

**Which is better for the customer? It depends on the customer.**

- **The monthly plan** keeps the cost visible, so people who are not using the gym notice and can cancel early. It also keeps them going, which is the reason they joined.
- **The annual plan** is usually cheaper per month, so it suits people who are sure to use it.
- **The worst case** for the customer is an annual plan with automatic renewal that they forget about. They pay for a year they do not use.

For the firm, the honest approach is to encourage use in every period, as Gourville advised, rather than to profit from members who forget.

## Concept 6: The decoy effect
### Definition, Huber, Payne and Puto (1982)

> Adding an option that is clearly worse than one product, but not worse than the other, shifts choice towards the product it makes look good.

The added option is the **decoy**. It is not meant to sell. It is also called the attraction effect or asymmetric dominance.

Note:
**The definition.** Adding an option that is clearly worse than one product, but not clearly worse than another, shifts choice towards the product it makes look good.

**The names.**

- The added option is the **decoy**. It is not meant to sell.
- The option it makes look good is the **target**.
- The other option is the **competitor**.
- The effect is also called the **attraction effect** or **asymmetric dominance**.

**Asymmetric dominance explained.** "Dominated" means worse on every feature, or worse on some and equal on the rest. The decoy is dominated by the target, but not by the competitor. So the domination is one-sided, or asymmetric.

**A simple example.** Two coffees: small ₹150 and large ₹250. Add a medium at ₹240. The medium is only ₹10 cheaper than the large but much smaller. It is clearly worse than the large. Next to it, the large looks like a bargain. Sales of the large rise, though few people buy the medium.

## The decoy effect: the theory
### Target T, competitor C and decoy D

| Option | Quality | Price | Compared with T | Compared with C |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| T, target | High | High | | Trade-off |
| C, competitor | Low | Low | Trade-off | |
| D, decoy | Lower than T | Same as T | **Clearly worse** | Trade-off |

D is dominated by T but not by C. So T looks like the obvious choice.

Note:
**What this slide shows.** The structure of a decoy, with three options: target T, competitor C and decoy D.

**Row by row.**

- **T, the target:** high quality, high price.
- **C, the competitor:** low quality, low price.
- **D, the decoy:** lower quality than T at the same price as T.

**Comparing T and C.** There is a trade-off. T is better but costs more. C is cheaper but worse. Without a decoy, buyers are split, and choosing is hard.

**Comparing D with T.** D is clearly worse. It costs the same and offers less. No sensible buyer picks D over T.

**Comparing D with C.** There is still a trade-off. D is better quality but more expensive.

**The effect.** D is dominated by T but not by C. This gives buyers an easy reason to pick T: "T is obviously better than D." The comparison between T and D is easy. The comparison between T and C is hard. People choose the option that wins the easy comparison.

**The key insight.** A decision is often not made by valuing each option alone. It is made by comparing options. Change the set of options, and you change the comparisons, and so the choice.

## Why the decoy breaks a rule
### The regularity principle

> Standard theory says adding a new option can never raise the share of an existing option. It can only take share away.

The decoy does the opposite. It raises the target's share without being chosen itself.

Note:
**What this slide shows.** Why the decoy effect matters for economic theory: it breaks the regularity principle.

**The regularity principle.** Standard choice theory says that adding a new option can never raise the share of an existing option. The new option can only take share away. If 40% choose A when A and B are offered, then at most 40% should choose A when A, B and C are offered.

**What the decoy does.** It raises the target's share, without being chosen itself. If 40% chose T before, 60% might choose T after D is added, and almost no one chooses D.

**Why this matters.** Regularity is one of the most basic properties of rational choice. If it fails, the value of an option is not fixed. It depends on what else is on the menu.

**The link to Module 3.** The decoy works like a reference point. It sets a local benchmark against which the target looks like a gain.

**For managers.** The menu itself is a tool. Options that no one buys can still change what people buy.

## The decoy effect in real sales
### Wu and Cosguner (2020), an online diamond retailer, *Marketing Science*

**The setting:** A leading online jewellery retailer listing thousands of diamonds, each with its price, carat, colour and clarity.

**The finding:** Buyers spotted a decoy next to the diamond that beat it only 11% to 25% of the time. When they did, the better diamond was 1.8 to 3.2 times as likely to sell.

**The value:** The researchers estimate that the decoy effect raised the retailer's gross profit by 14.3%.

Note:
**What this slide shows.** Evidence of the decoy effect in real sales, from Wu and Cosguner (2020), Marketing Science.

**Why this study matters.** Most decoy studies are in labs or classrooms, and some researchers doubt the effect in real markets. This study uses real purchase data.

**The setting.** A leading online jewellery retailer in the United States. It listed thousands of diamonds, each described by price, carat (size), colour, clarity and cut. This makes it possible to see when one diamond clearly beats another.

**The finding.** Buyers did not always notice decoys. They spotted a decoy next to the diamond that beat it only 11% to 25% of the time. But when they did notice one, the better diamond was 1.8 to 3.2 times as likely to sell.

**The value.** The researchers estimate that the decoy effect raised the retailer's gross profit by 14.3%.

**Two lessons.**

- The decoy effect is real in a market with real money and high stakes.
- It depends on the buyer seeing the comparison. Placing the decoy next to the target matters.

## Concept 7: The compromise effect
### Definition, Simonson (1989)

> An option gains share when it becomes the middle option in a set. People avoid the extremes.

The same product sells better when something more expensive is placed above it.

Note:
**The definition** of the compromise effect, from Itamar Simonson (1989).

**The definition.** An option gains share when it becomes the middle option in a set. People avoid the extremes.

**What this means in practice.** The same product sells better when something more expensive is placed above it. It does not need to change at all. It only needs a new neighbour.

**Examples.**

- Restaurants place a very expensive wine at the top of the list, so the second most expensive wine looks reasonable.
- Coffee chains offer three or four cup sizes, and the middle size sells most.
- Phone makers launch a "Pro Max" model above the "Pro", which makes the "Pro" look like the sensible choice.

**How it differs from the decoy effect.** A decoy is clearly worse than the target. In the compromise effect, the new option is not worse. It is simply more extreme. It changes which option sits in the middle.

## The compromise effect: the theory
### Extremeness aversion

|||

### Why the middle wins

Each extreme has a clear weakness: too basic or too costly. The middle is easy to justify to yourself and to others.

|||

### The link to Module 3

Moving from the middle to an extreme means losing on one attribute. Losses weigh about twice as much as gains.

Note:
**What this slide shows.** Why the middle option wins, known as extremeness aversion.

**Left column: why the middle wins.**

- Each extreme has a clear weakness. The cheapest option may be too basic. The most expensive may cost too much.
- The middle option is a safe choice. It avoids both weaknesses.
- It is easy to justify to yourself and to others. "I did not buy the cheapest, but I did not waste money either." Simonson argued that people choose options they can explain.

**Right column: the link to Module 3.**

- Think of each option as a set of features, such as quality and price.
- Moving from the middle to an extreme means gaining on one feature and losing on another.
- Because losses weigh about twice as much as gains, the loss looms larger. So people stay in the middle.

**For managers.** The position of a product in a range matters as much as its own features. Adding a top option can move buyers up. Removing the cheapest option can also move buyers up, because the old middle becomes the new bottom.

## The compromise effect: the evidence

## The compromise effect: an experiment
### Sharpe, Staelin and Huber (2008), soft drink sizes, *Journal of Consumer Research*

**The setting:** Buyers chose among soft drink cup sizes, with and without the smallest and largest sizes on offer.

**The finding:** Buyers avoided the smallest and the largest cup. People who chose 16 ounces when a 12-ounce cup was offered chose 21 ounces once the 12-ounce cup was dropped.

**The estimate:** The authors believe that dropping small sizes and adding larger ones has raised soft drink consumption by about 15%.

Note:
**What this slide shows.** Evidence on the compromise effect from Sharpe, Staelin and Huber (2008), Journal of Consumer Research.

**The setting.** Participants chose among soft drink cup sizes. The researchers changed which sizes were offered: sometimes the smallest size was removed, sometimes a larger size was added.

**The finding.** Buyers avoided the smallest and the largest cups. A buyer who chose 16 ounces when a 12-ounce cup was offered tended to choose 21 ounces once the 12-ounce cup was dropped. The 16-ounce cup had been the middle option. Once the smallest cup went, 16 ounces became the smallest, and the buyer moved up.

**The estimate.** The authors believe that the industry practice of dropping small sizes and adding larger ones has raised soft drink consumption by about 15%.

**Why this study matters.** It shows the compromise effect has real effects on health, not only on sales. The title of the paper is "Using extremeness aversion to fight obesity". The authors suggest a policy use: requiring small sizes on menus could reduce consumption without a tax.

**For managers.** The size range on a menu is a powerful tool. It can move customers up, but firms should think about whether that is in the customer's interest.

## You sell two laptop plans. Should you add a premium plan you expect few people to buy?

What could go wrong?

Note:
**About this slide.** This question applies the compromise effect to a product line decision. A firm sells two laptop plans and is thinking of adding a premium plan that few people will buy. The follow-up asks students to think about the risks, not only the gains.

**Answer. Usually yes, if the premium plan is a real product.**

- **The premium plan has its own value.** A few buyers will want it, and they bring high margins.

- **Buyers may see it as a trick.** If the premium plan has no real extra value, buyers notice, and trust falls.
- **Wrong buyers may move up.** Some may buy the premium plan and regret it, which raises returns and complaints.

- **Cost.** A third plan needs stock, support and training.
- **The basic plan may lose share,** which can hurt if it brings new customers into the brand.

## Concept 8: Assortment design
### Definition

> Assortment design is deciding how many options to offer, and how to group and present them.

Too few options, and buyers find nothing that fits. Too many, and some buyers give up. This is called **choice overload**.

Note:
**The definition** of assortment design: deciding how many options to offer, and how to group and present them.

**The trade-off.**

- **Too few options,** and buyers find nothing that fits their needs. They go elsewhere.
- **Too many options,** and some buyers give up. They find the choice too hard, or fear choosing wrongly, and choose nothing.

**Choice overload.** The second problem is called choice overload. More choice leads to fewer purchases, or to less satisfaction with what is bought.

**Why this matters for managers.** Many firms assume that more variety is always better. Each new product may bring a few new customers. But the total effect on the range may be negative if the extra options make choosing harder for everyone.

**Presentation matters too.** Assortment design is not only about the number of options. Grouping options into categories, using filters, or highlighting a "most popular" option can reduce the burden of a large range.

## Assortment design: the theory
### More choice has benefits and costs

|||

### Benefits

A better match for each buyer. A large display also attracts attention.

|||

### Costs

More effort to compare, more fear of regret, and more people who put off the decision.

Note:
**What this slide shows.** The benefits and costs of more choice.

**Benefits.**

- **A better match for each buyer.** With more options, each buyer is more likely to find one that fits exactly.
- **Attention.** A large display draws people in. It signals that the store is a specialist.

**Costs.**

- **More effort to compare.** Each extra option adds comparisons. With 6 options there are 15 pairs to compare. With 24 there are 276.
- **More fear of regret.** With many options, it is more likely that a better one was missed. This reduces satisfaction even after buying.
- **More people who put off the decision.** When choosing is hard, the easiest choice is not to choose. People delay, and delay often becomes "never".

**The link to Module 3.** Choosing one option means giving up all the others. With many options, the sense of loss from what was given up grows.

**The balance.** The best range is not the largest. It depends on how easy the options are to compare and how clear buyers' preferences are.

## Assortment design: the classic evidence
### Iyengar and Lepper (2000), a jam tasting stall in a California store

| | 24 jams | 6 jams |
|---|---|---|
| Shoppers who stopped | 60% | 40% |
| Of those, who bought | 3% | 30% |
| Buyers per 100 passers-by | 1.8 | 12 |

Note:
**What this slide shows.** The famous jam study by Iyengar and Lepper (2000), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology.

**The setting.** A tasting stall in an upscale grocery store in California. On some days the stall showed 24 jams. On other days it showed 6 jams. Shoppers who stopped got a discount coupon.

**The results, row by row.**

**Why the study became famous.** It challenged the belief that more choice is always better. It is cited in many books and business talks.

**A caution.** One study in one store is not enough. The next slide shows what happened when researchers looked at many studies together.

## Assortment design: a caution
### Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd (2010)

> A review of 63 conditions from 50 experiments, with 5,036 people, found an average choice overload effect of virtually zero, but large differences between studies.

So more choice is not always worse. Overload appears in some settings, such as hard comparisons and buyers without clear preferences.

Note:
**What this slide shows.** A meta-analysis by Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd (2010), Journal of Consumer Research.

**What a meta-analysis is.** It combines the results of many studies on the same question to find the overall effect.

**What they found.** They combined 63 conditions from 50 experiments, with 5,036 people in total. The average choice overload effect was virtually zero. But the results varied a lot between studies. Some found strong overload. Others found that more choice increased buying.

**What this means.** More choice is not always worse. Choice overload is real in some settings, but it is not a general law.

**When overload is more likely.**

- When options are hard to compare, for example with many features.
- When buyers do not have clear preferences, for example in a new product category.
- When the decision is complex or important, such as pensions or insurance.
- When there is time pressure.

**The lesson for managers.** Do not assume that cutting options will raise sales. Test it. The jam study is a useful story, but the evidence is mixed.

## Your app offers 40 plans. How would you redesign the menu?

**Payment decoupling: paying early or later hurts less.** The loss from paying is felt less when it is separated in time or form from the pleasure of use.

**Decoy effect: a worse option sells the target.** The comparison set creates the zero point. The target is judged against the decoy, and wins.

**Compromise effect: the middle wins.** Moving to an extreme means a loss on one feature. Loss aversion makes the middle safer.

**Assortment design: how many, and how grouped.** More options bring more effort and more potential regret. These costs can outweigh the benefit of a better match.

**The common thread.** These four concepts are about how firms design payments and menus. They work because buyers judge options relative to each other and relative to reference points, as prospect theory predicts.

**Taken together.** The module shows that the value of a rupee, a price or an option is not fixed. It depends on labels, timing and context.

## A checklist for any price, payment or menu decision
### Ask before you launch

1. Which **mental account** will the customer charge this to?
2. What is the customer's **reference price**, and is the one we show true?
3. Is the **full price** clear before the customer commits?
4. Does the **menu** help the customer choose, or push them where they would not go?

Note:
**What this slide shows.** A four-question checklist for managers making any price, payment or menu decision.

**1. Which mental account will the customer charge this to?** A treat, a necessity, a bill or a windfall? This affects how much they will spend and how they will feel. For example, a gift card is charged to a "treat" account.

**2. What is the customer's reference price, and is the one we show true?** The reference price shapes whether the price feels fair. If we show a "was" price or an MRP, it must be real.

**3. Is the full price clear before the customer commits?** Splitting prices is not wrong in itself. Hiding mandatory charges until checkout is drip pricing.

**4. Does the menu help the customer choose, or push them where they would not go?** Decoys, middle options and the number of options can help customers find what fits them. They can also steer customers to options that suit the firm but not the customer.

**How to use the checklist.** Each question links to one or more concepts. Together they cover the main ways pricing design can help or harm customers.

## The honesty test
### One question for every pricing idea

> Would we be comfortable if the customer saw exactly how and why we designed this price?

If yes, it is good design. If no, it is manipulation, and in India it may now be a dark pattern under the CCPA guidelines of 2023.

Note:
**What this slide shows.** A single test to judge whether a pricing idea is good design or manipulation.

**The question.** Would we be comfortable if the customer saw exactly how and why we designed this price?

**If yes,** it is good design. For example, offering a mid-range plan as a sensible choice, or showing the full price clearly with a separate delivery fee. A customer who knew the reasoning would not object.

**If no,** it is manipulation. For example, inventing a "was" price, hiding fees until the last step, or adding a fake option that cannot really be bought. A customer who knew the reasoning would feel cheated.

**The legal angle in India.** The CCPA Guidelines for Prevention and Regulation of Dark Patterns, 2023, list 13 dark patterns. They include false urgency, basket sneaking, drip pricing, subscription traps and disguised advertisements. Using these can now be treated as an unfair trade practice under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019.

**Why this matters.** Behavioural pricing tools are powerful. The honesty test helps managers use them in ways that build trust rather than destroy it.

## Exit quiz
### Five questions on the module

| | Question |
|---|---|
| 1 | Name the three parts of mental accounting. |
| 2 | Value ₹1,500, price ₹1,200, reference price ₹1,400. Find acquisition and transaction utility. |
| 3 | Why can a separate ₹40 delivery fee raise demand? |
| 4 | What makes an option a decoy? |
| 5 | Why did 6 jams sell more than 24? Does this always happen? |

Note:
**About this slide.** The exit quiz has five short questions, one for each of the main ideas of the module. It checks the four learning outcomes from the start of the module. Below are model answers.

**1. The three parts of mental accounting** (Thaler 1999):

- **Coding outcomes:** each gain or loss is judged with the value function.
- **Assigning to accounts:** money is put into labelled budgets, such as rent, food and fun.
- **Balancing accounts:** accounts are closed daily, weekly or per trip.

**2. Acquisition and transaction utility.**

Acquisition utility = value − price = 1,500 − 1,200 = ₹300

Transaction utility = reference price − price = 1,400 − 1,200 = ₹200

The buyer gains ₹300 from the product and feels a ₹200 good deal.

**3. A separate ₹40 delivery fee.** Buyers anchor on the base price and do not fully add the fee, so the total feels lower than it is (Morwitz, Greenleaf and Johnson 1998). The fee is also seen as a fair charge for a service. The effect is stronger when the fee appears late, which becomes drip pricing.

**4. What makes an option a decoy.** It is clearly worse than the target on at least one feature and no better on any, but it is not clearly worse than the competitor. It is not meant to sell. It makes the target look like the obvious choice.

**5. Six jams against 24.** With 24 jams, shoppers found it hard to compare and feared choosing the wrong one, so more of them gave up (3% bought, against 30% with six). This does not always happen. A review of 50 experiments found an average effect of about zero (Scheibehenne et al. 2010). Overload appears when the options are hard to compare and buyers have no clear preference.
