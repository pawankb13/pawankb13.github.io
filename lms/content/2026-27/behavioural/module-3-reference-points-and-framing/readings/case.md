### Kaveri Fibre: Pricing a Loss

Note:  Kaveri Fibre, its people and all figures are fictional. The case was written for class discussion only.
___

### A hundred rupees

On the morning of 2 September 2026, Prakash Gowda called Shalini Rao into his office and closed the door.

Gowda had founded Kaveri Fibre in 2016. Rao had joined as Head of Pricing eighteen months earlier, after six years in the pricing team of a national telecom company. She reported to Gowda directly.

He did not waste time. Kaveri's new bandwidth contract would start on 1 January 2027. It would add about ₹70 a month to the cost of serving each subscriber. The main plan, 100 Mbps for ₹799 a month, would no longer pay its way at that price. Gowda wanted it at ₹899.

"A hundred rupees," he said. "Our rivals already charge that. Our customers are sensible people. They will compare, they will see we are still good value, and they will stay."

Rao was less sure. She had run three sets of tests over the summer, and the results did not all point the same way. She also had a desk full of ideas from colleagues about how the rise should be announced. Some of them made her uncomfortable.

Gowda asked her to bring a full recommendation to the board meeting on 30 September. He wanted three things: whether to raise the price, how to announce it, and what else Kaveri should change at the same time.

"Do not bring me a price," he said as she left. "Bring me a plan."

### Kaveri Fibre

Kaveri Fibre was a fibre broadband provider based in Mysuru, Karnataka. Gowda, a network engineer, had started it in 2016 with a loan, two vans and a small team laying fibre in three residential areas of the city. By 2026 it served all of Mysuru and several nearby towns, including Mandya and Hunsur. It employed about 450 people, most of them field technicians.

Kaveri had 1,20,000 subscribers in August 2026. Most were households. The rest were students in rented rooms and small businesses such as shops, clinics and coaching centres. Almost all of them were on the main plan: 100 Mbps for ₹799 a month, including GST. The price had not changed since 2021. A Wi-Fi router came free with every connection.

Customers could pay monthly or buy an annual plan. The annual plan cost ₹9,588, which was twelve months at ₹799. Most customers paid monthly.

At ₹799 a month, the subscriber base brought in about ₹115.1 crore a year. Monthly churn, the share of subscribers who cancelled in a month, had been steady at about 1.8% for two years. At that rate, an average subscriber stayed for about four and a half years.

The market had changed since 2016. Two national providers now sold fibre broadband in Mysuru. Their comparable plans cost about ₹899 a month. Local cable operators were cheaper but slower and less reliable. Kaveri had built its name on local service. Its technicians usually fixed a fault on the same day, and many customers knew them by name.

Gowda was proud of that. He was also clear about its limits. "People like us," he told Rao in her first week. "That does not mean they will pay anything we ask."

### The cost problem

Kaveri bought most of its internet bandwidth from a large national carrier. The old contract ended in December 2026. The new one, signed in July, raised the price sharply. Kaveri's finance team estimated that it would add about ₹70 a month to the cost of serving each subscriber, or about ₹10.1 crore a year.

Gowda's arithmetic was simple. If all 1,20,000 subscribers paid ₹899 instead of ₹799, annual revenue would rise from ₹115.1 crore to ₹129.5 crore. That would cover the new cost with room to spare.

Rao pointed out that the arithmetic assumed nobody would leave. Gowda accepted that some would. He thought the number would be small, because ₹899 was what the national providers already charged.

Gowda had also suggested a way to soften the change. Kaveri could raise the price by ₹50 in January and another ₹50 in July. "Two small steps," he said. "Nobody notices fifty rupees."

There was a third idea on the table. Manjunath K., Kaveri's Head of Network, had told Rao that the fibre already in the ground could carry 200 Mbps to every home. Moving all customers from 100 Mbps to 200 Mbps would cost Kaveri very little. In a customer survey in May, households said they would value 200 Mbps at about ₹300 a month more than 100 Mbps.

Nobody had yet decided whether the speed upgrade should be part of the price rise, announced separately, or kept for later.

### What the tests showed

Rao had not wanted to guess how customers would react. Between March and August 2026 she ran three sets of tests.

#### The pilot towns

The first test ran from March to May in two towns of similar size. Each had about 6,000 subscribers on the ₹799 plan, and in each, monthly churn had been 1.80% for the past year.

In Hunsur, Kaveri cut the price by ₹50, to ₹749. In Mandya, it raised the price by ₹50, to ₹849. Nothing else changed. Over the three months, churn in Hunsur fell to 1.60%. Churn in Mandya rose to 2.25%.

Rao noticed that the two changes were the same size but had not moved churn by the same amount. The results are in Exhibit 2. Both towns returned to ₹799 in June.

#### The speed trial

The second test ran from June to August. Kaveri picked 20,000 customers at random and split them into two groups of 10,000.

The first group received an offer: upgrade to 300 Mbps for ₹200 more a month. Five per cent accepted.

The second group received 300 Mbps free for 30 days, with no action needed. At the end of the month, each customer was asked whether they wanted to keep 300 Mbps for ₹200 more a month or go back to 100 Mbps. Seventeen per cent chose to keep it.

Kaveri also surveyed both groups. It asked the first group the most they would pay each month for 300 Mbps. The median answer was ₹120. It asked the second group what monthly discount would make them willing to give 300 Mbps back. The median answer was ₹280. The results are in Exhibit 3.

#### The plan page survey

The third test, also from June to August, was a survey inside the Kaveri app. Kaveri picked 20,000 monthly customers at random. Each was shown one version of a draft plan page and asked: "If the monthly price rises to ₹899 in January, which plan would you choose?" On the draft page, twelve months on the monthly plan cost ₹10,788. The annual plan stayed at ₹9,588, the old price, a saving of ₹1,200 a year. The same page would be used for all renewals from January.

There were four versions, each shown to 5,000 customers. Two tested the wording of the message above the plans. One said "Choose annual and save ₹1,200 a year." The other said "Stay monthly and lose ₹1,200 a year." The other two tested which plan was already selected when the page opened: monthly or annual. Customers could switch with one tap either way. The results are in Exhibit 4.

Rao knew that a survey answer is not a payment. But the four groups were chosen at random and saw the same prices, so she was confident the differences between them were real.

#### The ideas on Rao's desk

By mid-September, Rao had a folder of proposals from her colleagues. Each one was about how to make the rise work. They did not agree with each other, and some of them she was not sure Kaveri should do at all.

#### From the CEO

Gowda wanted one price list for everyone. From 1 January, all customers, old and new, would pay ₹899. He saw no reason to treat existing customers differently. "New customers already compare us with the national providers at ₹899," he said. "Why should old customers be any different?"

He still liked his idea of two steps of ₹50, in January and July.

#### From finance

The finance team asked for a churn forecast. They needed to know how many customers Kaveri might lose after a ₹100 rise, for how long, and whether the rise would still pay after those losses. They suggested assuming that any extra churn would last about three months before settling back.

#### From network

Manjunath repeated his offer. Kaveri could move every customer to 200 Mbps on 1 January at almost no cost. He asked only to be told whether it would be announced with the price rise or on its own.

#### From marketing

Farah Ali, the Head of Marketing, sent four ideas.

First, she wanted the plan page to use the "lose ₹1,200 a year" message, because it had done better in the survey. She also wanted the annual plan selected by default.

Second, she proposed giving every customer 300 Mbps free for 30 days in February. At the end of the month, customers would be moved to 300 Mbps at ₹1,099 a month unless they opted out. "Most people never get round to opting out," she wrote. "That is the point."

Third, she had redesigned the monthly bill. The current bill showed one line for the plan, with separate lines for any late fee, reconnection fee or add-on. Her new bill listed each benefit of the January plan on its own line, with its value: the 200 Mbps speed, worth ₹300 a month; an OTT streaming pack that Kaveri could include at low cost, with a retail value of ₹149 a month; and the free router, which rivals rented out for about ₹100 a month. All charges would appear as one total.

Fourth, she wanted a promotion to soften the January rise. Option one was ₹10 cashback on every monthly bill. Option two was a monthly lucky draw: one customer in every 1,000 would win a free year of broadband, worth ₹10,788. The two options would cost Kaveri about the same.

#### From sales

Vikram Hegde, the Head of Sales, had a simpler idea. Kaveri's 300 Mbps plan would cost ₹1,099. He wanted the new ₹899 plan advertised as "Was ₹1,099, now ₹899". "It is the same speed class," he said. "Customers will feel they are getting a deal."

#### Two weeks

Rao read through the folder on a Sunday evening. Some ideas were clever. Some were probably effective. A few she suspected were both effective and wrong.

She had until 30 September to turn them into one plan. It had to work for the board, and she wanted it to be a plan she could also defend to Kaveri's customers.

## Assignment questions

Prepare short answers before class. Use the concepts from Module 3 and the numbers in the exhibits. Where a question asks for a calculation, show your steps one below the other.

1. Gowda says existing customers will compare ₹899 with the national providers, just as new customers do. Do you agree? Which customers will feel ₹899 as a loss, and which will not?
2. Use Exhibit 2 to compare the effect of a ₹50 cut with the effect of a ₹50 rise. What does the comparison tell you about Kaveri's customers?
3. Estimate monthly churn after a ₹100 rise. Using finance's three-month assumption, does the rise still pay?
4. Should Kaveri raise the price once by ₹100, or twice by ₹50? Should the 200 Mbps upgrade be announced together with the rise or separately? Write the announcement you would use.
5. Use Exhibit 4. Is the gap between 18% and 24% large enough to trust? Should Kaveri use the "lose ₹1,200" message?
6. Use Exhibit 3. Why did 17% keep 300 Mbps after a free trial, when only 5% accepted the direct offer? Compare the two survey medians. What would the trial be worth to Kaveri per year?
7.  Should the annual plan be selected by default? Under what conditions would that be fair to customers?
8. Do you support Farah Ali's new bill? Why does listing benefits one by one, and showing charges as one total, change how customers feel about the same money?
9. Cashback or lucky draw? The two cost about the same. Which would customers value more, and why? What would you check before running a lucky draw?
10.  Look again at the proposals in Exhibit 5. Which would you accept, which would you change, and which would you reject? For each one you reject, say which concept it misuses.
11. Write Rao's one-page recommendation to the board. State the price, how and when it will be announced, and what else Kaveri should change. Keep it to five points.
