1. Title — 0:20 (clock 0:20)

Good morning, everyone. Thank you for having me. My name is Pawan Kamble, and today I’ll present my paper, “Can Financial Literacy Alleviate Poverty? Rethinking the Link through a Multidimensional Lens.”

2. Overview — 0:10 (clock 0:30)

I’ll start with the motivation and the main findings, then briefly cover the data and methods, the results and robustness checks, and close with the policy implications.

Don’t read out the section list.

3. Introduction and Motivation (the Puzzle) — 1:00 (clock 1:30)

Let me start with a puzzle. Over the last decade, India ran one of the largest financial inclusion drives in the world. Through PMJDY, about 80% of Indians now have a bank account. But in 2019, roughly a third of account holders didn’t make a single deposit or withdrawal. The accounts exist — they just aren’t being used.

So the question behind this paper is: why hasn’t access translated into lower poverty?

Pause for one beat.

This matters because India has made real progress — multidimensional poverty fell from about 25% to 15%, and nearly 250 million people moved out of it. But the gains are uneven: Bihar, Jharkhand and UP still lag far behind.

Most of the policy response has come through large welfare schemes. They give essential short-term relief, but they address access — not the household’s own capacity to manage money and stay out of poverty.

4. The Research Problem — 0:45 (clock 2:15)

Two problems sit underneath this. First, poverty has traditionally been measured by income or consumption. But following Sen, and Alkire and Foster, poverty is really about deprivation — in health, education and living standards. Income alone misses that.

Second, the usual answer has been financial inclusion. But access is not empowerment. The Andhra Pradesh microfinance crisis showed that credit without financial understanding can push people into over-indebtedness. And the Global Findex continues to find behavioural and informational barriers, not just access gaps.

5. Conceptualization — 0:45 (clock 3:00)

This points to financial literacy as a possible missing link. The classic definition, from Lusardi and Mitchell, is narrow — knowledge of financial concepts and the ability to calculate. The OECD uses a broader definition, and that’s the one I follow. Financial literacy has three parts: knowledge — understanding interest, inflation and risk; attitude — how you think about money and the future; and behaviour — what you actually do: budgeting, saving, managing debt.

This distinction is central to the paper. And it matters in India, where only about 27% of adults are financially literate.

6. What This Paper Shows — 1:45 (clock 4:45)

So, coming back to the puzzle: if access isn’t enough, financial literacy is the natural candidate. But the real question is — which part of it matters?

Three findings. First, a one–standard-deviation increase in financial literacy is associated with a 0.08 standard-deviation reduction in multidimensional poverty. When I instrument for financial literacy, the effect rises to about 0.31 standard deviations.

Second — and this is the main result — it’s attitude, not knowledge, that drives the effect. With all three components in the model, financial attitude has more than twice the effect of knowledge, and behaviour adds little on top.

Third, the effects are largest for women and rural households — exactly the groups policy most wants to reach.

Now, an obvious reaction is: isn’t this just saying educated people are less poor? Three reasons it isn’t. I control for education, so this effect is over and above schooling. I measure poverty as deprivation, not income — so the mechanism isn’t that literate people earn more; it’s that they use what they have better. And I address reverse causality with an instrument, and sample selection with a Heckman correction.

So the one-line takeaway: policy teaches what people know about money — but poverty responds to how they think about it.

Say the last line slowly. It’s the sentence you want people to remember.

7. Literature — 0:50 (clock 5:35)

The paper sits at the intersection of three literatures. On poverty, the multidimensional approach from Sen and Alkire–Foster, now used officially by NITI Aayog. On financial literacy, the move from a knowledge-only view to the broader OECD view. And on the link between them, Churchill and Marisetty show that financial inclusion reduces multidimensional poverty in India.

Each has a gap, shown in the bottom row. Studies of financial literacy and poverty mostly use income. Most financial literacy studies measure knowledge only. And there is little causal evidence that separates the components. This paper addresses all three.

8. Methods Overview — 0:20 (clock 5:55)

I use the 2016 Financial Inclusion Insights survey — a nationally representative survey of adults across 22 Indian states. I build two indices, estimate OLS, then use IV and Heckman models for causality and selection.

9. Empirical Strategy — 0:35 (clock 6:30)

For the outcome, I follow Churchill and Marisetty and keep the MPI sub-dimensions in the survey that relate to a household’s financial situation, keeping the original framework of three dimensions and their weights.

One concern: the usual education indicator — the household head’s education — is likely correlated with the respondent’s own financial literacy, through cognitive ability.

10. Data — 0:35 (clock 7:05)

To deal with this, I use the education of the woman in the household — the female head, or the spouse of the male head, who is not the respondent. That way the respondent’s own ability isn’t counted on both sides of the equation.

This reduces the sample to about 35,700, and the reduced sample may not be random — which I address later with a Heckman model.

11. MPI Construction — 0:45 (clock 7:50)

The poverty index has the standard three dimensions, each weighted one third. Health: delaying a doctor visit, or going without enough food, because of lack of money. Education: the woman’s education, and any school-age child not attending school. Living standards: six household assets.

Each indicator equals one if the household is deprived, so the score runs from 0 to 1, and higher means poorer. For the regressions I standardise it as a z-score.

12. Financial Knowledge — 0:25 (clock 8:15)

For financial literacy, knowledge uses the five standard questions — inflation, interest, simple interest, compounding and risk diversification. One point per correct answer, so the score is out of five.

13. Financial Attitude — 0:25 (clock 8:40)

Attitude uses four Likert-scale statements — financial satisfaction, comfort with debt, confidence in one’s own skills, and attitude towards investing. They’re coded so a higher value means a more positive attitude, then averaged on a 0-to-4 scale.

14. Financial Behaviour — 0:25 (clock 9:05)

Behaviour uses seven items about what people actually do — planning, saving, paying bills on time, spending less than they earn, having savings larger than debt, keeping an emergency fund, and investing. Score out of seven.

15. Composite Index — 0:25 (clock 9:30)

Because the three components are on different scales, I standardise each one and the composite. So every coefficient I show is the effect of a one-standard-deviation change in financial literacy on poverty, also in standard deviations.

16. Regression Model — 0:50 (clock 10:20)

The baseline model regresses the poverty index on financial literacy, controls and state fixed effects. Controls cover gender, age, marital status, education, employment, religion, rural–urban location and economic shocks. State fixed effects absorb anything shared within a state — policy, infrastructure, average income — so the comparison is between people in the same state. Standard errors are clustered at the village or town level, matching the survey design.

Model 2 replaces the overall index with its three components together, so each coefficient shows one component’s effect holding the other two fixed.

17. Results — 1:30 (clock 11:50)

Here are the main results. Column 1: a one-SD increase in financial literacy lowers poverty by 0.08 SD, significant at 1%.

Columns 2 to 4 enter each component separately — all three reduce poverty. But look at Column 5, where all three are entered together. Attitude stays large, at −0.073. Knowledge falls to −0.034. Behaviour almost disappears, at −0.012.

So knowledge matters, but attitude matters more than twice as much. And once you account for attitude, behaviour adds very little — consistent with attitude being the upstream driver that shapes behaviour.

Point to Column 5 while you say this. It’s the most important number in the talk.

18. Important Controls — 0:35 (clock 12:25)

The controls behave as expected. Economic shocks increase poverty substantially; rural households are poorer; and education has a strong protective effect — tertiary education lowers poverty by about 0.8 SD. Financial literacy matters even after holding all of this constant.

✂️ If behind: skip this slide with one line: “The controls behave as expected; details are in the table.”

19. Heckman Selection Model — 0:50 (clock 13:15)

First robustness check: selection. Because I needed the woman’s education, I lost about 6,600 observations. Using the full sample of about 42,300, I estimate a Heckman model, with household size and farm ownership as exclusion restrictions.

The inverse Mills ratio is significant, so selection does exist. But the results barely change — financial literacy is −0.089, and attitude still dominates at −0.068 against −0.034 for knowledge.

20. IV Setup — 0:40 (clock 13:55)

Second, endogeneity. Poorer people may have fewer chances to become financially literate, and unobserved ability may drive both. So, following Churchill and Marisetty, I instrument financial literacy with time to reach a bank — whether it’s within 30 minutes. Living near a bank means repeated exposure to financial institutions, products and programmes, which builds financial literacy.

21. IV Results — 1:05 (clock 15:00)

The instrument is strong: in the first stage, living near a bank raises financial literacy by about 0.23 SD, and the Kleibergen–Paap F-statistic is 478 — far above the threshold of 16. The Durbin–Wu–Hausman test rejects exogeneity, so financial literacy is endogenous and the IV estimate is the one to rely on.

The second-stage effect is −0.31 SD — about four times the OLS estimate. That’s consistent with OLS being biased toward zero by measurement error in the index, and with the IV capturing the effect for people whose literacy depends on bank access — likely poorer, more rural households with the most to gain.

22. Heterogeneity — 1:00 (clock 16:00)

Finally, who benefits most? The effect is larger for women than men — −0.094 versus −0.070 — and much larger in rural areas — −0.091 versus −0.051. For financial attitude specifically, the effect is about 44% larger for women and 50% larger for rural households.

One more pattern: the effect is larger for the formally educated, −0.10 versus −0.04. Financial literacy seems to complement education rather than replace it.

23. Discussion — Why Does Attitude Dominate? — 0:45 (clock 16:45)

Why does attitude matter most? Attitude comes before behaviour. Following Ajzen’s theory of planned behaviour, future orientation and financial confidence are what turn knowledge into action. Someone can know what compound interest is and still not save — attitude is the bridge. And it promotes the habits that protect households: careful credit use, formal saving and insurance.

24. Transmission Mechanism — 0:45 (clock 17:30)

How does this reduce deprivation? Through each of the three dimensions. In health, through insurance and avoiding large medical expenses. In education, through budgeting and investing in children’s schooling, which lowers dropout. And in living standards, through formal savings, building assets and resilience to income shocks.

✂️ If behind: combine slides 23 and 24 into about 45 seconds.

25. Implications — 0:50 (clock 18:20)

Four policy implications. First, redesign financial literacy programmes to focus on attitude and behaviour, not just knowledge and calculation. Second, embed financial literacy in existing welfare schemes, so it reaches the households who need it most. Third, target women and rural households, where the returns are highest. And fourth, measure success differently — not by the number of accounts opened, but by active use: saving, insurance and managing debt.

26. Conclusion — 0:50 (clock 19:10)

To conclude. Financial literacy significantly reduces multidimensional poverty in India — 0.08 SD in OLS and 0.31 SD with IV — and the result holds after correcting for selection. The main driver is attitude, not knowledge, and the largest gains are for women and rural households.

So, back to the puzzle we started with. Access to a bank account isn’t enough. Financial literacy isn’t a substitute for poverty policy — it’s what makes poverty policy work.

27. Thank You — 0:10 (clock 19:20)

Thank you. I’m happy to take questions.

Likely questions (keep answers to about 30 seconds)

“Why is the IV estimate four times the OLS estimate?”
Measurement error in a survey-based index biases OLS toward zero. The IV is also a local effect, for people whose literacy depends on bank proximity — likely poorer, rural households with larger returns.

“Doesn’t bank proximity affect poverty directly?”
That’s the key assumption. I condition on rural–urban location and state fixed effects, which absorb much of the remoteness and infrastructure difference. Bank proximity mainly matters through exposure to financial institutions and information. I’d acknowledge it as a limitation and mention that a test using a second instrument is a natural next step.

“Isn’t attitude just a reflection of being better off — reverse causality?”
Partly possible, which is why the IV matters. Also, the attitude items are about orientation (debt comfort, investing, confidence), and the result holds within state, controlling for education, employment and shocks.

“Why is the Male coefficient positive?”
Be ready for this one (+0.119 on the controls slide). One likely reason: when the respondent is a man, the education indicator refers to his spouse, so men and women respondents are measured against different household members. Check your own data before the talk.

“Data from 2016 — is it still relevant?”
It’s the most recent nationally representative survey with all three financial literacy components. 2016 is also when PMJDY’s account expansion was at its peak, which makes it the right moment to study the gap between access and use.
